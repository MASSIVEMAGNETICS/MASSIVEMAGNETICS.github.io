## Abstract

Human beings possess a strictly finite and non-storable lifetime, yet many modern systems account precisely for money, output, labor cost, engagement, and machine performance while only crudely accounting for whose time is consumed, whose objectives that time serves, and who controls the time surplus created by technology. Existing scholarship already provides major foundations: economic theories of time allocation, time-use surveys, national time accounting, time-poverty research, work-life balance research, attention economics, subjective well-being measures, and productivity studies. This paper does not replace those traditions. It proposes an additional systems-level accounting layer centered on temporal sovereignty: a person's practical ability to control the allocation, use, preservation, and benefits of finite lifetime.

The paper introduces a key distinction: time saved is not necessarily time returned. Automation may reduce the human effort technically required for a task while the resulting surplus is reassigned as additional work, captured by a platform, consumed by maintenance and coordination, or fragmented by attention costs. To measure this gap, the paper proposes Net Human Time Return (NHTR), Sovereign Time Return (STR), Temporal Return on Investment (TROI), Temporal Debt, and a provisional Human-Time Farming Index (HTFI). It also proposes a Temporal Ledger for AI and automation systems and a set of falsifiable experiments comparing conventional productivity metrics against agency-adjusted time-return metrics.

The central hypothesis is not that human time has been ignored. It is that modern measurement systems often fail to track the disposition and control of time surplus with the same rigor used to track financial surplus. If supported empirically, temporal accounting could become a useful complement to productivity, GDP, engagement, labor, and well-being metrics in AI governance, workplace design, software engineering, creator economics, public policy, and human-computer interaction.

> **PRIMARY CONTRIBUTION:** TIME SAVED ≠ TIME RETURNED. The proposed accounting question is: after a technology reduces required human effort, what fraction of the resulting temporal surplus is actually restored to the human as discretionary, agency-preserving time?

## Research standard

This repository deliberately makes a narrow claim. Existing economics, time-use research, labor studies, attention research, HCI, and well-being scholarship already establish that time and agency matter. The proposed contribution is to operationalize the **disposition and ownership of technology-created time surplus** as an auditable variable and test whether it adds predictive or decision value beyond conventional productivity and engagement metrics.

## Core proposed metrics

- **Time Saved (TS)** — reduction in direct human time for an equivalent verified outcome.
- **Net Human Time Return (NHTR)** — savings net of interaction, maintenance, rework, coordination, recovery, and attention costs.
- **Sovereign Time Return (STR)** — NHTR adjusted by the fraction of surplus genuinely controlled by the human.
- **Temporal Return on Investment (TROI)** — future sovereign human time returned per unit of human time invested.
- **Temporal Debt** — expected future mandatory human time created by present systems or decisions.
- **Human-Time Farming Index (HTFI)** — provisional measure of systematic time capture, optimization, monetization/value conversion, and asymmetry.

## Falsifiability

The framework should be revised or rejected if these temporal variables do not improve prediction, project selection, automation evaluation, agency measurement, or real-world decision quality beyond simpler existing metrics.
